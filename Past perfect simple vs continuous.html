Past Perfect,
made logical.

You already know how to talk about the past. Now learn how to move further back in time — and how to choose between a completed result and an activity in progress.

The big idea

Two past perfect forms. One past reference point.

Both forms look backwards from a moment in the past. The key question is what you want to focus on.

earlier pastpast reference pointnow
Simple
result / completion / earlier event
Continuous

1. Anchor the timeline

Past perfect only makes sense when there is another past moment to look back from. Start with the chronology, not with the verb form.

STEP 01

Event A — earlier

The bus left.

This happened first.

Event B — later

We arrived.

This is the past reference point.

Combine them

The bus had left when we arrived.

Had left pushes Event A further back.

💡
Think “past before past”. The past perfect does not simply mean “a long time ago”. It marks a relationship between two past points.

2. Past Perfect Simple

Use it when you want the listener to see an earlier action as completed, achieved, counted, or already true before another past moment.

STEP 02

Form

had + past participle (V3)
She had finished the report.full affirmative
She’d finished the report.contracted affirmative — ’d = had
She hadn’t finished the report.contracted negative — hadn’t = had not
Had she finished the report?question

Use it for…

completed earlier actions
By the time I called, he had left.
results / achievements
She was relieved because she had passed the exam.
experience before a past point
He had never flown before that trip.
states before a past point
They had known each other for years.
🗣️
Contractions in real English. After a pronoun, had is often shortened to ’d: I’d, you’d, he’d, she’d, it’d, we’d, they’d. The negative is usually hadn’t. Be careful: ’d can also mean would. Compare She’d finished (= she had finished) with She’d help (= she would help). The structure after ’d tells you what it means.

Build the form

Switch the sentence type and watch the word order. Try the contracted version too.

3. Past Perfect Continuous

Use it when the important idea is the activity itself: how long it had been happening, what had been going on, or what caused a visible past result.

STEP 03

Form

had been + verb-ing
They had been waiting for an hour.full affirmative
They’d been waiting for an hour.contracted affirmative — ’d = had
They hadn’t been waiting long.contracted negative
Had they been waiting long?question

Use it for…

duration up to a past point
She had been studying for three hours when I called.
an activity still in progress
He had been reading when the lights went out.
a cause with a past result
The pitch was muddy because it had been raining.
⚠️
State verbs usually stay simple. Verbs such as know, believe, understand, like, own normally do not take the continuous form: They had known each other for years, not had been knowing.

4. Simple or continuous?

The two forms often describe the same time relationship. What changes is the speaker’s focus.

STEP 04

Past Perfect Simple

Focus: the result

Was it finished?
Do we care about how many / how much?
Is it a state rather than an activity?
VS

Past Perfect Continuous

Focus: the activity

Do we care about how long?
Was it in progress up to that point?
Did the activity cause a past result?

Compare the meaning

Maya had written three pages before lunch.Result/count: three pages were complete.
Maya had been writing for two hours before lunch.Activity/duration: the process matters.
Tom had repaired the bike, so we could use it.Completed result: the bike was fixed.
Tom was covered in oil because he had been repairing the bike.Past evidence: the activity explains the result.

5. Story mission: The locked classroom

Use chronology and meaning, not isolated signal words. Each clue tells you whether the story needs a completed earlier event or an ongoing activity.

STEP 05
🔐

Monday, 7:55 a.m.

Your class arrived for a test, but the classroom door was locked. The teacher looked exhausted, the floor outside was wet, and the printed tests were already on the desk inside.

Clue 1: The tests were already printed.
Clue 2: The teacher was exhausted.
Clue 3: The corridor floor was wet.

FINAL
One-minute takeaway

Ask two questions.

1. What is my past reference point?

If another event happened before it, a past perfect form may be needed.

2. What is my focus?

Result / completion / state → Simple.
Activity / duration / cause → Continuous.
